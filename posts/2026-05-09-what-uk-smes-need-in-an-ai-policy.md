If your team is already using ChatGPT, Copilot or any other AI tool, even informally, you probably need an AI policy. Not a forty-page legal document, but a clear, written agreement about how AI gets used in your business, what it can and can't do, and who's responsible when things go wrong.

When we first wrote this in May, most small businesses were using AI to draft things. Now plenty are letting it do things: chase invoices, reply to reviews, answer the phone. That changes what a policy needs to cover, and the rules have moved too, so we've updated this guide.

## What's changed since May

**UK law now allows more automated decisions, with safeguards.** Since 5 February 2026, the Data (Use and Access) Act has replaced the old near-ban on fully automated decisions about people. If AI makes a significant decision about someone with no real person involved (screening a job applicant, say, or setting their price), you must tell them, let them have their say, offer a person to review it, and let them challenge it. A person who just clicks "approve" on whatever the AI says doesn't count. Stricter rules still apply to health and other sensitive data.

**The ICO is writing an AI code of practice.** The UK's data regulator is now required by law to produce a code on AI and automated decisions. No draft has been published yet, but it's coming, and a business that has already written down what its AI does will be in a much better place when it lands.

**The EU AI Act's toughest rules have been pushed back to December 2027.** For most small businesses up here it only matters if you sell into the EU. If you do, one part already applies: people must be told when they're interacting with AI.

None of this is a reason to panic. However, it is a good reason to have something in writing.

## What a proportionate AI policy should cover

For most small businesses, a page or two covering these six areas is enough.

### 1. Acceptable use

Be clear about which AI tools your team can use and what for. You might allow ChatGPT, Copilot or Claude for drafting, summarising and research, while keeping anything involving sensitive customer data or money decisions off limits.

A simple rule of thumb: if you wouldn't be happy with it going out under your business name without someone reading it first, it doesn't go out without someone reading it first.

### 2. Data handling

This is the most important section for data protection. Free AI tools often use what you type to improve their models unless you switch that off, and some now show adverts too. Business plans generally give you stronger protection.

Your policy should say:

- What never goes into an AI tool (customer details, financial information, anything personal or sensitive)
- Which tools the business has approved, and why
- Who to ask if someone isn't sure

### 3. Accuracy and human review

AI makes mistakes. It makes up facts, misreads context and sounds confident while doing it. Anything AI produces for customers, whether that's an email, a quote, a proposal or a post on your website, should be read by a person before it goes out. That's the deal.

### 4. AI that does jobs on its own

This is the new one. If AI is chasing invoices, replying to reviews or answering calls, your policy should say:

- Which jobs it can do by itself, and which need a person to sign off first
- What it's never allowed to do (agree a refund, promise a date, make a decision about someone's job or credit)
- Who checks what it's been doing, and how often

The sensible starting point is that AI drafts and a person approves. Once you trust a job, you can loosen the reins, but write down that you've done it and why.

### 5. Being upfront with customers

Decide how you'll tell people when they're dealing with AI. If an AI answers your phone or web chat, say so at the start and give people an easy way to reach a person. If AI is making a significant decision about someone, the safeguards above apply. For content like emails and posts, there's no single right answer, but pick a position and stick to it.

### 6. Intellectual property

Who owns AI-generated content is still a grey area in UK law. Don't rely on AI alone where originality really matters, and be aware that AI output may not be protected by copyright in the way your own work is.

## What you don't need

You don't need to ban AI. Bans are almost impossible to enforce, and your team will use these tools anyway, just without any guidance on doing it safely.

You also don't need to cover every possible scenario. Focus on the tools your team actually uses today and the jobs AI is actually doing for you.

## A starting point

If you haven't written anything yet, start with a short paragraph for each of the six areas above. Talk it through with your team, and look at it again every six months, or sooner if the ICO's code lands.

*This is a plain English guide, not legal advice. If you're using AI to make significant decisions about people, get the safeguards right and take specialist advice where you need it.*
